The discussion begins as [[Socrates]] employs his logical scrutiny to challenge conventional, unthinking views of morality, like [[Cephalus]]’s focus on honesty and debt repayment.

The conversation escalates with the sophist [[Thrasymachus]], who launches a "robust assault on convention". His famous claim is that **"Morality is nothing other than the advantage of the stronger party"**. Thrasymachus asserts that the immoral life, especially when practiced on a large scale, possesses **"more power, license, and authority than morality"**.

Socrates attempts a rebuttal using the Craft Analogy, arguing that true expertise (like a shepherd or doctor) seeks the good of its subject, not the practitioner’s profit. However, by the end of the chapter, Socrates admits he still hasn't grasped the nature of morality itself.

I think the failure of this first chapter is important because it validates the challenge that follows. Thrasymachus forces the defense of morality out of the abstract and into a discussion of power and self-interest. Socrates’ commitment to reason here prepares the foundation for the entire philosophical structure of the city.
